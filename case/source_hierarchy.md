# Source Hierarchy

All extracted facts must be tagged with source strength and source type. Higher tiers are not all doing the same job. Some sources establish legal obligations, some prove communications or events, some show party allegations, and some only support strategy or research.

AI-generated summaries are never evidence.

## Tier 1A - Operative Court Authority and Official Court Records

Examples:

- final decree
- signed court orders
- temporary orders
- commissioner recommendations if operative or applicable
- docket entries
- minute entries
- official hearing orders

Use:

- controls legal obligations and procedural posture
- can establish what the court ordered, found, scheduled, or adopted
- strongest source for what the operative order says

## Tier 1B - Filed Pleadings and Court-Filed Party Documents

Examples:

- file-stamped motions
- oppositions
- replies
- declarations
- filed exhibits
- proposed orders

Use:

- proves that a party made an assertion, request, or representation to the court
- useful for procedural history, prior positions, admissions, and consistency analysis
- not proof that factual assertions inside the pleading are true unless independently supported, admitted, or adopted by the court

Rule:

A filed pleading proves that a party made an assertion. It does not prove that the assertion is true.

Exhibits attached to pleadings should be classified by their underlying source type, not automatically elevated just because they were filed.

## Tier 2A - Neutral / Third-Party Contemporary Records

Examples:

- medical records
- school records
- provider communications
- billing records
- portal records
- police reports if applicable
- evaluator records if applicable

Use:

- strong support for what a neutral or third-party source recorded
- often strong on dates, notices, attendance, diagnoses actually recorded, recommendations, and billed services
- still should be read carefully for scope and hearsay layers

## Tier 2B - Party Communications and Platform Records

Examples:

- OFW messages
- emails between parties
- text messages
- parent communication logs

Use:

- strong for proving what was said, when, and by whom
- helpful for notice, refusals, admissions, logistics, and timing
- not automatic proof that every statement inside the message is true

## Tier 3 - Attorney Communications, Drafts, and Strategy Materials

Examples:

- attorney emails and correspondence
- draft pleadings
- strategy memos
- mediation proposals
- attorney notes

Use:

- useful for understanding strategy, procedural history, negotiation posture, and drafting intent
- privileged or strategy-sensitive in many cases
- not source evidence unless independently supported

## Tier 4 - User Notes, Recollections, and Informal Summaries

Examples:

- personal journal entries
- contemporaneous notes
- case summaries
- recollections of events

Use:

- useful for issue spotting, chronology building, and follow-up questions
- should usually be corroborated before being used as factual support in a draft
- label clearly as party recollection, user note, or informal summary

## Tier 5 - AI-Generated Content

Examples:

- document cards generated by this tool
- fact candidates extracted by this tool
- timeline entries generated by this tool
- review findings and synthesis

Use:

- organizational aids only
- never evidence
- every AI-generated factual statement must trace back to a real source document or be flagged as unsupported

## Legal Authority Is Not Evidence

Legal authority supports legal propositions, not factual propositions.

Examples:

- statutes
- rules
- case law
- local rules
- administrative rules
- legal research memos

Legal authority should be handled by legal-authority-scholar or by supplied legal materials. It may support propositions such as what standard applies, what remedy may exist, or what procedure may be available. It does not prove who said what, when an exchange occurred, or whether a party actually complied.

### Legal Authority Hierarchy

1. Binding primary authority
2. Persuasive primary authority
3. Secondary authority
4. Supplied legal research
5. Unverified assumptions

If currentness or citator status has not been checked, say so explicitly.

## Key Rules

1. Never cite an AI summary as a source. Trace to the underlying document.
2. Distinguish party allegations from established facts.
3. Court orders can establish obligations and findings. Pleadings usually establish positions, not truth.
4. Contemporaneous records usually beat later recollections.
5. Party communications prove the communication itself better than the truth of every embedded claim.
6. Exhibits inherit the strength of their underlying source type.
7. Legal authority is not factual evidence.
8. Flag disputed facts explicitly.
